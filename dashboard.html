    What each country's trade press is discussing that others are not

    A phrase is listed under a country when that country's trade press accounts for far more of its coverage than that country's share of the whole corpus would predict. The comparison is against the corpus, not raw counts — otherwise every phrase would look American simply because most feeds are. Country means where a publication is based, not what an article is about: a US paper writing about China is US coverage. Presence and absence only — nothing here says one country used a phrase before another.

    Regulators, agencies and standards bodies named in coverage

    Institutions are cross-sector by nature — a regulator does not belong to one sector — so several sectors naming the same body is a different kind of observation from a phrase spreading. Names are read from article summaries, where capitalisation means something; headlines are often title case and would make every phrase look like a name. Articles without a summary contribute nothing, so this undercounts rather than guesses.

    What the conversation turned toward this fortnight

    A broad phrase says little on its own — "data center" could be a property story, a power story or a chip story. What sits beside it is what makes it specific. This is every companion phrase that is new or rising this fortnight, across all tracked topics, ranked by how much it grew. It describes what is being discussed, not what happens next.

    Which sectors are taking on new vocabulary

    Phrases that arrived in each sector's coverage for the first time, by when they arrived. A sector absorbing several at once is in flux; one carrying many but absorbing none has settled. Nothing else here answers this — it is the closest thing to a single read on where language is moving.

            How the mix of sectors has shifted · weekly

            Band height is how many of this phrase's articles came from each sector that week. Bands stack, so the outline is total coverage and the slices are who was writing it. A phrase changing hands looks like one band thinning while another swells — sequence, not cause: nobody knows whether one sector took the phrase from another or both responded to the same thing.

            Roughly what share of each sector's SEC filings mention it · since 2016

            Ten years of the same question the spread map asks over ten weeks, from company disclosures rather than press coverage. Sector labels are the SEC's own. Quarterly and formal, so it lags events — a company writes about a constraint after the trade press has covered it. The denominator is estimated from several near-universal phrases rather than counted exactly, so read the shape and the crossovers rather than the exact percentages.

            Every figure here is a count of articles actually read. Which phrases appear in more than one sector's coverage, which sector used each one first, and how usage has moved over the past fortnight. Nothing is modeled, estimated or inferred — the articles are linked so you can check any of it.
            It makes no forecast. Testing found that neither a phrase accelerating nor spreading across sectors predicted what followed, so scores and historical analogs were removed rather than shown with caveats. "Rising" describes the last fortnight, not the next one.
