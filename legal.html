What this is not

Crosstalk is not investment advice. It counts how often phrases appear in

trade publications. It makes no recommendation to buy, sell or hold anything,

and it makes no forecast.

Testing was run three times on whether language spreading between sectors

precedes price movement — against public attention data, against link-sharing

data, and against ten years of SEC filings compared with sector prices. All

three found nothing. The last used 868 events across 42 quarters and returned a

breadth premium of −1.1 points at p = 0.62.

Nothing here is a signal. Decisions taken on the basis of this site are yours.

No warranty. The site is provided as it is. Feeds break, publishers change

their formats, and extraction makes mistakes. Figures may be wrong.

What is collected about you

Nothing.

There are no accounts, no sign-in, no cookies, no analytics, no advertising and

no tracking of any kind. The site is a static file served by GitHub Pages; there

is no server that could record anything.

Your watchlist is stored in the address bar of your own browser, in the part

after the #. It is never sent anywhere. Copying the link is what shares it;

closing the tab is what deletes it.

GitHub, who host the site, keep their own server logs. That is outside our

control and covered by their privacy policy.

If you subscribe to the weekly digest, your email address is held by Buttondown,

who send it. It is used to send the digest and nothing else. Unsubscribing

removes it.

What is stored about articles

Headlines, summaries, links, publication names and dates. Never article text.

Every publication is read through the RSS feed it publishes for that purpose.

What a feed provides — a headline and a short summary — is what is stored, along

with the link back to the original.

Full article text is not fetched, not stored and not shown. Every figure on the

site links to the publisher's own page, and reading the article means going

there.

Phrases are counted, not reproduced. A phrase counted here is two or three

words.

Publications

Publishers are named throughout, and every article links to its source. If you

publish one of the feeds read here and would rather not be included, write to

contact@crosstalkwire.com and it will be removed.

Copyright

The analysis, the site and the code are the operator's own. Headlines, summaries

and article links belong to the publishers who wrote them.

How to read the figures

Every figure is a count of articles actually read. Words crossing between sectors now, and how usage moved over the last 14 days. Nothing is modeled, estimated or inferred — the articles are linked so you can check any of it. Where a phrase appears in a summary a publisher syndicated, that sentence is quoted beneath the headline; the publisher's own page may since have been rewritten or put behind a paywall.

Which companies appear. A company is listed when it is named alongside a phrase that started in a different sector. A utility discussed using utility words tells you nothing; a carmaker discussed using materials words is the point. Firms written about only in their own sector's vocabulary are left out however often they are mentioned — this is not a coverage tracker.

What a crossing means. A phrase leaving its home sector means people in another sector have decided it concerns them too — a supply problem becoming a policy question, a technical constraint becoming a financial one. It says attention has moved. It does not say what will happen next, and nothing here is investment advice.

It makes no forecast. Testing found that neither a phrase accelerating nor spreading across sectors predicted what followed. “Rising” describes the last 14 days, not the next one.
